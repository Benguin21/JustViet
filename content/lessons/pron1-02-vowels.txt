BOARD v1: ba | three
TUOI explain: Let’s begin with [a], [ă] and [â]. For [a], open your mouth comfortably wide.

TUOI: It’s roughly like the vowel in English “father,” although English accents vary. Play [ba].

BOARD v2: ăn | to eat
TUOI explain: [ă] is also an open vowel, but it’s shorter. You’ll hear it with a consonant after it, as in [ăn].

BOARD v3: ân | kindness, favor
TUOI explain: For [â], bring your tongue a little higher and make a short, central vowel. A short “uh” is a rough starting point.

CLEAR
WORDS v4:
  an | safe, peaceful
  ăn | to eat
  ân | kindness, favor
TUOI: Now compare all three. Listen for the longer open vowel, the shorter open vowel, and the short central vowel.

POINT v4: north
VINH: Try my Northern button, then Cô Tươi’s Southern button. The exact vowels may differ a little between our accents.

VINH encourage: Use your chosen voice as your model when you repeat.

TUOI ask: Are [a], [ă] and [â] just the same vowel with different tones?
  WRONG: Yes, only the tone changes. | These are three separate vowel letters, not tone marks. Replay the board and compare the vowels.
  RIGHT: No, they are different vowel letters. | Exactly! The letter changes the vowel. A tone mark does a different job.

CLEAR
BOARD v5: me | tamarind
TUOI explain: Next are [e] and [ê]. For [e], open your mouth a little wider. It’s roughly like the vowel in English “bed.”

BOARD v6: mê | to be fascinated by
TUOI explain: For [ê], close your mouth a little more and raise your tongue. It’s somewhat like the start of English “say.”

TUOI: Hold the vowel steady. Don’t slide into an extra “ee.”

WORDS v7: me, mê
TUOI: Play the pair and notice how your mouth closes a little for [ê]. Compare both of our recordings.

CLEAR
WORDS v8:
  đi | to go
  y tá | nurse
TUOI explain: Now [i] and [y]. When either one is the whole vowel, both can sound like “ee.” Listen to the vowel in each word.

WORDS v9:
  i ngắn | “short i”
  y dài | “long y”
TUOI: You may hear [i] called [i ngắn] and [y] called [y dài]. Those names tell the two letters apart.

TUOI: They don’t mean you should stretch [y] longer than [i]. Play the names to practice them.

TUOI explain: Don’t swap [i] and [y] freely, though. Their roles in vowel combinations differ, and words have established spellings.

CLEAR
BOARD v10: to | big
TUOI explain: Our next family is [o], [ô] and [ơ]. For [o], round your lips and keep your mouth fairly open.

TUOI: Think of an open “aw”-like sound, then use the recording as your guide.

BOARD v11: tô | bowl
TUOI explain: For [ô], keep your lips rounded, but close your mouth a little more. It’s roughly like “oh,” without the English glide at the end.

BOARD v12: tơ | silk thread
TUOI explain: For [ơ], relax your lips so they aren’t rounded. Think of an “uh”-like vowel, then copy the recording.

CLEAR
WORDS v13: to, tô, tơ
TUOI: Compare the three. The first two use rounded lips; the third doesn’t. Try both buttons, then repeat with your chosen voice.

WORDS v14:
  cân | to weigh
  cơn | a bout, a spell
TUOI explain: Remember [â], our short central vowel? [ơ] is related in sound but usually longer. Compare [cân] and [cơn].

CLEAR
BOARD v15: thu | autumn
TUOI explain: Our final pair is [u] and [ư]. For [u], round your lips as if you’re saying “oo.”

BOARD v16: thư | letter, mail
TUOI explain: For [ư], keep your tongue high and toward the back, but relax your lips. It’s a new mouth position for many English speakers.

WORDS v17: thu, thư
TUOI: Play the pair. Notice the rounded lips for [u] and the unrounded lips for [ư].

TUOI ask: Which vowel do we make with unrounded lips?
  WRONG: [u] | Replay the pair. Make [u] first, then relax your lips without moving your tongue much. That brings you toward [ư].
  RIGHT: [ư] | Yes! [u] uses rounded lips. For [ư], relax that rounding.

TUOI encourage: You’ve met all twelve vowel letters! Twelve letters doesn’t mean twelve different sounds. Remember [i] and [y]?
